The Catacombs of Kom El Shoqafa

Descend into Alexandria's most extraordinary necropolis: A 2026 Complete Guide to the underground world where Egyptian, Greek, and Roman cultures merge in death. Discover secrets, photography tips, and exclusive tour access.

Home/ Resources/ Catacombs of Kom El Shoqafa 2026
Updated June 20, 2026 35 min read Archaeology By Hossam, Egyptologist & Photographer

Beneath the bustling streets of modern Alexandria lies one of the ancient world's most astonishing archaeological discoveries: The Catacombs of Kom El Shoqafa. This sprawling necropolis, carved from solid bedrock in the 2nd century CE, represents the last great flowering of ancient Egyptian funerary art while simultaneously embodying the Greco-Roman influences that defined Alexandria during its cosmopolitan heyday. If you are fascinated by Alexandria's rich history, be sure to explore our Alexandria Day Trip Guide and our deep dive into Roman Ruins in Egypt.

As you descend the spiral staircase into the cool, dimly lit chambers, you enter a realm where Anubis wears Roman armor, where Egyptian burial rites mingle with Greek mythological scenes, and where the artistic traditions of three great civilizations fuse into something entirely unique. This comprehensive 2026 guide will illuminate every aspect of this remarkable site, from its accidental discovery by a donkey in 1900 to the photography techniques that best capture its eerie atmosphere today.

Chapter 1: The Accidental Discovery: A Donkey's Plunge into History

For centuries, the Catacombs of Kom El Shoqafa lay forgotten beneath Alexandria's soil, their existence erased from memory until one fateful day in September 1900. The story of their rediscovery reads like an archaeological adventure tale: a donkey pulling a cart stumbled upon unstable ground, plunged approximately 12 meters down an access shaft, and inadvertently revealed one of Egypt's most important historical sites.

Origin of the Name: "Mound of Shards"

The name Kom El Shoqafa translates directly from Arabic as "Mound of Shards" or "Mound of Pottery Fragments." This peculiar name originated from the enormous piles of broken terracotta vessels that accumulated at the entrance over centuries of use. Ancient visitors would bring food and wine to funeral banquets held in the catacombs but considered it bad luck to bring the empty containers back to the world of the living. Instead, they deliberately smashed them at the site, creating the characteristic "mound of shards" that would eventually give the catacombs their name.

Chapter 2: Architectural Marvel: Navigating the Three-Tiered Necropolis

The Catacombs of Kom El Shoqafa represent a sophisticated feat of underground engineering. The complex consists of three primary levels carved directly into the bedrock, connected by a central shaft around which a spiral staircase descends. This layout served both practical and symbolic purposes, facilitating the movement of bodies while representing the descent into the underworld.

Architectural Layout of Kom El Shoqafa
Level Depth Key Features Primary Function
Upper Level 0-10 meters Entrance staircase, vestibule with benches, initial burial niches Reception area, initial descent, simpler burials
Middle Level (Main) 10-20 meters Rotunda with central well, Triclinium, main burial chamber, Hall of Caracalla Primary ceremonial spaces, family burials, commemorative feasts
Lower Level 20-30 meters Additional burial chambers, loculi, originally water-filled areas Extended burial capacity, possibly later additions

The Central Shaft and Spiral Staircase

The descent begins with a remarkable spiral staircase approximately 6 meters in diameter that winds around a central cylindrical shaft. This shaft served the practical function of lowering bodies into the deeper levels using ropes, but its design also carries symbolic weight. The staircase features larger steps at the beginning that gradually become smaller as one descends—a design characteristic of Roman period construction that may have ritual significance related to the journey into the underworld.

Chapter 3: Astonishing Cultural Fusion: Where Three Civilizations Meet in Death

What truly sets the Catacombs of Kom El Shoqafa apart is their extraordinary synthesis of Egyptian, Greek, and Roman artistic traditions and religious concepts. This fusion was neither haphazard nor superficial but represents a deliberate and sophisticated blending of cultural elements that reflected Alexandria's unique identity as a Mediterranean crossroads.

Egyptian Tradition
Anubis, Osiris, mummification rituals, hieratic poses, solar discs, falcon symbolism.
Greek Influence
Persephone myth, Medusa heads, Agathodaemon serpents, banquet traditions, artistic naturalism.
Roman Elements
Architectural forms, military garb on deities, portrait hairstyles, sarcophagus decorations.

Case Study: Anubis the Roman Legionary

One of the most striking examples of cultural fusion appears in the depiction of Anubis, the Egyptian jackal-headed god of mummification and the afterlife. In the catacombs, Anubis is portrayed wearing the uniform of a Roman legionary—complete with breastplate, spear, and shield—while performing traditional Egyptian mummification rites. This remarkable hybrid captures the essence of Alexandrian syncretism: maintaining Egyptian religious concepts while adopting the visual language of Roman authority.

Chapter 4: Chamber by Chamber: Exploring the Catacombs' Key Spaces

The Rotunda and Central Well

Upon descending the spiral staircase, visitors enter the Rotunda, a circular chamber crowned with a dome supported by eight pillars. At its center lies a well approximately 8.5 meters deep, which originally served both practical and ceremonial functions. Archaeologists discovered five alabaster statue heads in this well (now replaced by casts, with originals in the Greco-Roman Museum), possibly representing deities or the tomb's patrons.

The Triclinium: Funeral Banquet Hall

To the left of the Rotunda lies the Triclinium, a rectangular banquet hall measuring approximately 9 by 8.5 meters. This space hosted funeral feasts where family and friends of the deceased would gather on stone benches (originally covered with cushions) to share ritual meals. The practice of holding commemorative banquets at burial sites has roots in Greek tradition but was enthusiastically adopted in Roman Egypt.

The Main Burial Chamber

The heart of the catacombs is the principal burial chamber, accessed through an entrance flanked by remarkable hybrid statues and protected by serpentine guardians. This chamber contains three massive rock-cut sarcophagi with non-removable lids, each decorated with garlands, bulls' heads, and gorgon masks that blend Roman decorative motifs with Egyptian funerary symbolism.

The Hall of Caracalla: Mystery of the Bones

Perhaps the most enigmatic space in the catacombs is the Hall of Caracalla, named for the Roman emperor who visited Alexandria in 215 CE. This chamber gained its name from the discovery of numerous human and animal bones within it, which early excavators connected to a massacre Caracalla allegedly ordered against the Alexandrian populace. However, more recent analysis suggests many of the bones belong to horses, specifically racehorses, as this section was dedicated to Nemesis, goddess of fortune and sports.

Chapter 5: Photography Masterclass: Capturing the Catacombs' Atmospheric Depths

The Catacombs of Kom El Shoqafa present extraordinary opportunities for photography but come with significant technical challenges. The combination of extremely low light, complex shadows, reflective surfaces, and restricted movement spaces requires careful preparation and technique. As Egypt's premier photography tour specialists, we've developed proven strategies for capturing stunning images in these unique conditions. Always remember to follow ethical practices; read our Ethical Photography Guide for more.

Essential Equipment & Settings

  • Fast Wide-Angle Lens: 16-35mm f/2.8 or wider is ideal for capturing the chambers in low light.
  • High ISO Performance: You'll be shooting at ISO 1600-6400 regularly. Shoot in RAW to recover shadow details.
  • Available Light: The catacombs have strategically placed artificial lighting. Use it to your advantage to create dramatic shadows and silhouettes.
  • No Flash: Flash photography is strictly prohibited as it damages ancient pigments.

Chapter 6: 2026 Visitor Guide: Practical Information for Your Visit

For the 2026 season, the Catacombs of Kom El Shoqafa maintain the following schedule (subject to change; verify before visiting):

  • Opening Hours: Daily from 8:00 AM to 5:00 PM (last admission typically at 4:30 PM)
  • Admission Fees (2026): Approximately 80 Egyptian Pounds for adults, 40 Egyptian Pounds for students with valid ID.
  • Physical Accessibility: Steep spiral staircase, uneven floors, low doorways. Not recommended for those with mobility issues. Wear sturdy shoes.
  • Climate: Constant cool temperature (approx. 18-20°C), higher humidity. Bring a light jacket.

Important Visitor Guidelines

  • No touching of reliefs, carvings, or walls: Oils from skin accelerate deterioration.
  • Absolutely no flash photography: Flash damages ancient pigments and is strictly prohibited.
  • Respect the space as a burial site: Maintain a quiet, respectful demeanor.
  • Follow designated pathways: Off-limits areas are restricted for preservation and safety.

Frequently Asked Questions

What makes the Catacombs of Kom El Shoqafa so unique?
The Catacombs of Kom El Shoqafa are unique because they represent an extraordinary fusion of Egyptian, Greek, and Roman artistic styles and funerary traditions all within one site. Built in the 2nd century CE during Roman rule, the catacombs feature Egyptian deities depicted with Roman armor, Greek mythological scenes alongside Pharaonic burial motifs, and architectural elements that blend all three cultures seamlessly.
How were the Catacombs of Kom El Shoqafa discovered?
The catacombs were discovered completely by accident on September 28, 1900, when a donkey drawing a cart fell into an access shaft approximately 12 meters deep. This accidental discovery revealed the entrance to the long-forgotten necropolis.
What are the must-see features inside the catacombs?
Key features include the spiral staircase descending around a central shaft, the Rotunda with its dome and central well, the Triclinium (banquet hall), the main burial chamber with three rock-cut sarcophagi, the Hall of Caracalla, and the unique statues blending Egyptian poses with Roman hairstyles.
Is photography allowed inside the Catacombs of Kom El Shoqafa?
Yes, photography is generally permitted, but flash photography is strictly prohibited as it can damage the ancient artwork. Our specialized Alexandria photography tours provide expert guidance on capturing the catacombs' atmospheric interiors using available light techniques and fast lenses.
Do Egypt Photography Tours include visits to the Catacombs?
Absolutely! We offer specialized Alexandria day trips that include the Catacombs of Kom El Shoqafa as a highlight. Our 7-Day Egypt Discovery Tour and private tours provide comprehensive access with an Egyptologist guide who explains the cultural fusion and historical context.
