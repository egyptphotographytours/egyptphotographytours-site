Valley of the Kings: A Guide to the Tombs of the Pharaohs (Tutankhamun, Ramses)

The Valley of the Kings — the very name evokes mystery, treasure, and the eternal glory of ancient Egypt's pharaohs. Nestled on the west bank of the Nile opposite modern Luxor, this arid landscape hides the final resting places of Egypt's New Kingdom rulers — including the most famous pharaoh of all, Tutankhamun, and the mighty Ramses II. For over 500 years (c. 1539–1075 BCE), the kings of the 18th to 20th Dynasties carved their tombs deep into the Theban hills, hoping to safeguard their bodies and belongings for the afterlife. Chapter 1: The History of the Valley of the Kings

The Valley was chosen for its remoteness and the natural pyramid-shaped peak of Al-Qurn, which the ancients revered. Pharaohs like Thutmose I broke with tradition by separating their tomb from their mortuary temple, hiding their burial deep in the cliffs. The Valley is split into the East Valley (where most tombs lie) and the West Valley. Over 63 numbered tombs (KV1–KV65) have been discovered — from simple pits to the sprawling KV5 with over 150 chambers, likely built for the sons of Ramses II.

The Sacred Peak
The pyramid-shaped mountain Al-Qurn was sacred to the goddess Meretseger. Tombs were aligned symbolically, and the peak's shadow was believed to protect the necropolis.
Royal Innovation
Ineni, architect of Thutmose I, wrote: "I supervised the excavation of the tomb of His Majesty… no one seeing, no one hearing." Secrecy was paramount.
Tomb Robberies
Most tombs were looted by the end of the New Kingdom. Priests rewrapped and moved many royal mummies to secret caches like DB320 for protection.

KV Numbers and Mapping

The "KV" (Kings' Valley) numbering system was established by John Gardner Wilkinson in 1827. KV1 to KV65 are used, with gaps for tombs that were later renumbered or never found. The most recent discoveries, KV63 and KV64, were found in 2005 and 2012 — proving the Valley still holds secrets.

Chapter 2: Tomb Architecture and Decoration

Tombs evolved from simple corridor tombs (like Thutmose III) to complex, multi-chambered structures with deep shafts and pillared halls. The layout symbolized the journey of the sun god Ra through the underworld. Walls are covered with texts like the Amduat, Book of Gates, and Litany of Re, guiding the pharaoh through the night toward rebirth.

Chapter 3: The Tombs of the Pharaohs

KV62 – Tomb of Tutankhamun

Discovered nearly intact by Howard Carter in 1922, KV62 is the most famous tomb in the world. Though small (likely a private tomb hastily adapted for the young king), it contained over 5,000 artifacts. Today, the mummy of Tutankhamun remains on display in the burial chamber, while the treasures moved to the Egyptian Museum (and now the Grand Egyptian Museum). Visiting KV62 is to step into history — the decorated walls show Ay, his successor, performing the "Opening of the Mouth" ceremony.

KV7 – Tomb of Ramses II (Ramses the Great)

One of the largest tombs, KV7 is badly damaged by floods and ancient reuse. It stretches over 160 meters with a complex layout. Excavations by a French team continue; it's rarely open to the public due to instability, but our private tours can sometimes arrange special access with permissions.

KV11 – Tomb of Ramses III

Often cited as the most beautifully decorated tomb open to visitors. The reliefs are vibrant, depicting scenes from the Book of Gates and the Book of the Divine Cow. The sarcophagus still sits in the burial chamber. A must-see for any visitor.

KV17 – Tomb of Seti I (Father of Ramses II)

The longest and deepest tomb (over 137 meters), with exquisite raised reliefs. Currently closed for conservation, but our educational tours bring it to life through exclusive photo archives and expert storytelling.

Top 5 Tombs to Visit in 2026

  • KV62 (Tutankhamun) – Historical aura and the mummy itself.
  • KV11 (Ramses III) – Best-preserved colorful reliefs.
  • KV9 (Ramses VI) – Vast chambers with a stunning astronomical ceiling.
  • KV2 (Ramses IV) – Complete funerary texts, easy access.
  • KV8 (Merenptah) – Multiple sarcophagi, impressive depth.

Note: Only a limited number of tombs are open on rotation to preserve them. Our guides ensure you see the best available combination based on your interests.

Photographing the Tombs — Know the Rules

Photography is strictly prohibited inside most tombs to protect the paintings from flash damage. Here's how we handle it on our tours:

  • We teach low-light techniques in the few permitted areas (like entrance corridors).
  • We arrange permits for professional shoots where regulations allow.
  • We capture the exteriors and surrounding landscape with expert composition tips — the Valley's golden-hour cliffs are spectacular.

Chapter 4: Visiting the Valley of the Kings

Opening hours: 6:00 AM – 5:00 PM (winter), 6:00 AM – 6:00 PM (summer). Arrive early to beat the heat and crowds. Tickets are purchased at the visitor center; standard tickets allow entry to three tombs, excluding Tutankhamun and Seti I, which require separate tickets.

  • Footwear: Wear comfortable walking shoes — the paths are gravelly.
  • Electric train: Take the small train from the visitor center to the tomb entrances to save energy for the tombs themselves.
  • Water: Bring your own — kiosks exist but charge premium prices.
  • Flashlight: A red-filtered light helps you appreciate details without risking damage to the paintings.

Chapter 5: Conservation and the Future of the Valley

The Theban Mapping Project and the Getty Conservation Institute work tirelessly to monitor humidity, flash floods, and tourism impact. Some tombs are permanently closed to the public, but our limited-availability research-focused visits let you see conservation efforts in action and understand why responsible tourism matters here.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many tombs are in the Valley of the Kings?
There are 63 known tombs and chambers in the Valley of the Kings, designated KV1 to KV65 (some numbers unused). They range from simple pits to complex underground palaces like KV5 (over 150 chambers).
Can you take photos inside the tombs?
Photography is strictly prohibited in most tombs to protect the paintings from flash damage. However, Egypt Photography Tours offers special access workshops where permitted, and we coach you on low-light techniques in allowed areas.
Which tomb of Ramses is best to visit?
KV11 (Ramses III) is the most popular due to its well-preserved colorful reliefs. KV7 (Ramses II) is damaged but atmospheric; KV8 (Merenptah) and KV6 (Ramses IX) are also excellent. Our guides help you choose based on your interests.
Is Tutankhamun's tomb worth seeing?
Yes, for its historical significance and the famous mummy inside. Although small and less decorated, the experience of standing where Carter found the treasure is unforgettable. The treasures are in the Egyptian Museum.
What is the best time to visit the Valley of the Kings?
Early morning (opening at 6 AM) or after 2 PM to avoid crowds and heat. Winter months (Nov-Feb) are ideal. Our private tours secure early access to beat the queues.
Do Egypt Photography Tours include the Valley of the Kings?
Absolutely. Our 7-Day Discovery, Luxor extensions, and private photography tours include expert-guided visits to KV, with priority entry, detailed explanations, and photography coaching in permitted zones.
