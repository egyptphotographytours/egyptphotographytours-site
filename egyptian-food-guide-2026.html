Egyptian Food Guide 2026: 17 Dishes You MUST Try

Egyptian food represents one of the world's oldest continuous culinary traditions, with recipes and techniques dating back to the Pharaonic era over 5,000 years ago. Unlike many ancient food traditions lost to time, Egyptian cuisine has evolved while keeping its distinctive character — influenced by Greek, Roman, Arab, Ottoman, and French civilizations, yet always unmistakably Egyptian. This 2026 guide walks you through 17 must-try dishes, street food secrets, regional specialties, and how to photograph it all. Pair it with our Ultimate Egypt Packing List and Baksheesh Tipping Guide for a fully prepared trip.

Modern Egyptian cuisine is built on legumes (fava beans and lentils), fresh vegetables, aromatic herbs, and bold spices. Bread — 'aish baladi' — accompanies virtually every meal; the word 'aish' literally means "life" in Arabic. The Egyptian table is a communal experience, with dishes designed to be shared among family and friends.

Chapter 2: 17 Essential Egyptian Dishes You MUST Try in 2026

From bustling Cairo street corners to sophisticated Alexandrian seafood restaurants, these 17 dishes represent the heart and soul of Egyptian cuisine. Each tells a story of history, region, and cultural tradition perfected over centuries.

1. Koshari (Koshary)

Cairo & Nationwide

National DishVegetarianStreet Food

Egypt's undisputed national dish — a magnificent symphony of rice, brown lentils, chickpeas, and macaroni topped with spicy tomato sauce, garlic vinegar, and crispy fried onions. Originally an affordable worker's meal, it's now beloved across all social classes. Where to try it: Abu Tarek in Downtown Cairo — a multi-level temple to koshari that holds the world record for the largest koshari ever made.

Best Time: Lunch or late dinner
Price: 20–50 EGP ($0.65–$1.60)
Photo Tip: Capture the colorful layers before mixing
Tour: Cairo & Giza 2-Day Tour

2. Ful Medames

Nationwide Breakfast

VegetarianStreet Food

Egypt's breakfast of champions — slow-cooked fava beans seasoned with garlic, lemon, olive oil, and cumin, served with fresh 'aish baladi'. Simmered overnight in copper pots, the beans develop a creamy, comforting texture. Customize with tahini, boiled eggs, tomatoes, or clarified butter. More than breakfast — it's Egyptian resilience and resourcefulness in a bowl.

Best Time: Breakfast (6–11am)
Price: 10–25 EGP ($0.30–$0.80)
Photo Tip: Steam rising from copper street carts
Tour: 7-Day Egypt Discovery

3. Ta'ameya (Egyptian Falafel)

Cairo & Alexandria

VegetarianStreet Food

Egypt invented falafel — but the Egyptian version, ta'ameya, uses soaked fava beans (not chickpeas) mixed with parsley, coriander, leek, and spices, formed into flat discs and fried to crispy perfection. The distinctive grassy-green color and earthy flavor make it unique. Served in pita with tahini, salad, and pickles.

Best Time: Breakfast or lunch
Price: 15–30 EGP ($0.50–$1.00)
Photo Tip: Golden discs bubbling in hot oil
Tour: Alexandria Day Trip

4. Mulukhiyah

Nationwide, Especially Rural Areas

Vegetarian Option

Egypt's iconic green soup made from jute plant leaves, giving it a distinctive viscous texture newcomers quickly learn to love. Dates to Pharaonic times when it was considered food fit for royalty. The secret is the 'taqliya' — a final sizzling of garlic and coriander poured over the soup just before serving. Served over rice with chicken or rabbit.

Best Time: Lunch or dinner
Price: 40–80 EGP ($1.30–$2.60)
Photo Tip: Vibrant green against white rice
Tour: Family Tours home dining

5. Hamam Mahshi (Stuffed Pigeon)

Cairo & Rural Areas

Luxury DishMeat

Egypt's most luxurious traditional dish — young pigeons stuffed with fragrant rice or freekeh mixed with spices, liver, and nuts, then grilled or baked. The birds are bred in distinctive conical pigeon towers across the countryside. Often served at weddings and celebrations, it represents Egyptian hospitality at its finest. Traditionally eaten with your hands.

Best Time: Special-occasion dinners
Price: 150–300 EGP ($5–$10)
Photo Tip: Whole presentation before carving
Tour: Honeymoon Tours romantic dinner

6. Kofta and Kebab

Nationwide

Meat

The pinnacle of Middle Eastern grilling. Kofta is spiced minced meat shaped around skewers and grilled over charcoal; kebab refers to chunks of marinated meat cooked similarly. The secret is the spice blend — cumin, coriander, onion, garlic, and parsley. In Luxor, Al Sayeda Zainab Restaurant is renowned for its deliciously spiced kofta.

Best Time: Lunch or dinner
Price: 60–150 EGP ($2–$5)
Photo Tip: Grilling action with charcoal flames
Tour: Private Pyramids Tour

7. Fiteer (Egyptian Savory Pastry)

Throughout Egypt

Vegetarian Options

Egypt's answer to pizza and pastry combined — a flaky, multi-layered bread stretched paper-thin, brushed with samneh (clarified butter), and baked. Sweet with honey, or savory with cheese, meat, or vegetables. Watching fiteer masters stretch the dough until nearly transparent is a culinary performance in itself.

Best Time: Any time of day
Price: 25–80 EGP ($0.80–$2.60)
Photo Tip: Video the dough-stretching process
Tour: Cairo & Giza Tour demo

8. Shawarma

Every Egyptian City

MeatStreet Food

The iconic street food lunch — marinated meat on a vertical rotisserie, shaved into pita with garlic sauce (toum), pickles, and sometimes fries. In Cairo, Kazaz near Tahrir Square is legendary for both chicken and beef shawarma. The best stands have lines extending down the block at lunch hours.

Best Time: Lunch or late dinner
Price: 30–60 EGP ($1–$2)
Photo Tip: Meat spinning on the vertical spit
Tour: Food Photography Tours

9. Mahshi (Stuffed Vegetables)

Nationwide Family Dish

Vegetarian

Egyptian home cooking at its finest — zucchini, eggplant, peppers, tomatoes, vine leaves, or cabbage stuffed with fragrant rice, herbs, and sometimes minced meat, slowly cooked in tomato broth. A labor of love reserved for family gatherings. Seasonal vegetables define each version — vine leaves in summer, cabbage in winter.

Best Time: Weekend family lunches
Price: 50–120 EGP ($1.60–$4)
Photo Tip: Colorful variety on a large platter
Tour: Family Photography Tours

10. Sayadiyah (Fish with Rice)

Alexandria & Coastal Cities

SeafoodCoastal Specialty

Egypt's premier seafood dish — firm white fish layered with golden onion-infused rice and baked in tomato-based sauce. The name means "fisherman's dish." The key is deeply caramelized onions. Enjoy it at Corniche restaurants in Alexandria with Mediterranean views.

Best Time: Lunch or dinner
Price: 80–200 EGP ($2.60–$6.50)
Photo Tip: Layered presentation before serving
Tour: Alexandria Day Trip

11. Fattah

Nationwide, Especially Upper Egypt

MeatFestive Dish

Egypt's ultimate celebratory dish, served at weddings, Eid, and major family gatherings — layers of crispy fried bread, rice, and tender lamb or beef soaked in garlic-vinegar broth and topped with tomato sauce. With Nubian origins, its name comes from the Arabic for "crumbling," referring to how the bread is broken.

Best Time: Special occasions & holidays
Price: 70–150 EGP ($2.30–$5)
Photo Tip: Dramatic unveiling of the layers
Tour: 7-Day Discovery Tour

12. Baba Ghanoush

Throughout Egypt

Vegetarian

A smoky, creamy eggplant dip that far surpasses supermarket versions. Charred eggplants mashed with tahini, garlic, lemon, and olive oil. The Egyptian version is smokier and more garlic-forward — the key is charring over an open charcoal flame. Served as part of a mezze spread with warm pita.

Best Time: Appetizer before the main meal
Price: 25–50 EGP ($0.80–$1.60)
Photo Tip: Charcoal-grilling whole eggplants
Tour: Private Tours mezze tasting

13. Besarah

Alexandria & Nile Delta

Vegetarian

Egypt's vibrant green bean and herb dip — a creamy puree of dried fava beans, parsley, dill, cilantro, and leeks, topped with crispy fried onions. Mentioned in medieval Arabic cookbooks, it's a traditional Alexandrian breakfast that's gained nationwide popularity as a healthy, protein-rich option.

Best Time: Breakfast or appetizer
Price: 20–40 EGP ($0.65–$1.30)
Photo Tip: Bright green puree vs. golden onions
Tour: Alexandria coastal tour

14. Hawawshi

Throughout Egypt

MeatStreet Food

Egypt's meat-stuffed pastry — spiced minced beef or lamb inside pita-like bread, baked in a stone oven until crispy outside and juicy inside. The traditional baking method (not frying) is what sets it apart. Each region adds its own twist — Alexandria favors more chili, Cairo more onion and cumin.

Best Time: Lunch (12–3pm)
Price: 25–50 EGP ($0.80–$1.60)
Photo Tip: Cross-section showing the juicy filling
Tour: Cairo Street Food Tour

15. Alexandrian Kebdah (Liver)

Alexandria & Major Cities

MeatStreet Food

Egypt's most beloved offal dish — calf's liver quickly sautéed with garlic, chili, cumin, and sometimes tomatoes, served in a sandwich with tahini and pickles. The high-heat technique achieves caramelization while keeping the interior tender. A bold, protein-packed option that converts even liver skeptics.

Best Time: Lunch or late-night snack
Price: 30–60 EGP ($1–$2)
Photo Tip: The sizzling cooking process
Tour: Alexandria Culinary Tour

16. Shakshuka

Alexandria & Coastal Areas

Vegetarian

A hearty breakfast of eggs poached in spicy tomato, pepper, and onion sauce, served in the cooking pan with fresh bread for dipping. Egypt's version packs more chili and garlic than regional cousins. Seaside cafés along the Alexandria Corniche serve it with Mediterranean views.

Best Time: Breakfast or brunch
Price: 40–80 EGP ($1.30–$2.60)
Photo Tip: Top-down shot of eggs in red sauce
Tour: Alexandria Day Trip

17. Traditional Nubian Tagine

Aswan & Nubian Regions

MeatCultural Specialty

A taste of Egypt's southern culinary traditions — meat (often camel, chicken, or lamb) slow-cooked with vegetables and spices in a clay pot over coals. Camel tagine is particularly sought-after for its lean, slightly sweet flavor. On Elephantine Island in Aswan, King Jamaica's restaurant serves a version many travelers call their favorite meal in all of Egypt.

Best Time: Dinner
Price: 80–200 EGP ($2.60–$6.50)
Photo Tip: Traditional clay cooking pots
Tour: 7-Day Discovery Aswan segment

Chapter 3: The Ultimate Egyptian Street Food Experience

Egyptian street food isn't just sustenance — it's a cultural experience, a social activity, and an art form. From dawn until late night, the streets come alive with vendors offering everything from breakfast ful to midnight shawarma.

Street Food Safety Guidelines

  • Follow the crowds: the busiest vendors have the freshest food and highest turnover.
  • Watch the cooking: choose places where food is prepared fresh to order.
  • Stick to hot foods: cooked-to-order items are safer than pre-prepared cold ones.
  • Bottled water only: never drink tap water or accept ice in drinks.
  • Vegetable caution: be careful with raw vegetables that may have been washed in tap water.
  • Trust your guide: our guides know the safest, most authentic vendors.

Must-Try Street Food Extras

  • Fresh sugar cane juice: Egypt does it better than anywhere — less earthy, more refreshing. Look for shacks with hanging cane stalks.
  • Luqmat al-Qadi: freshly fried dough balls drenched in honey or syrup, sometimes chocolate-covered.
  • Street pizza (Fatayir): pita-like breads topped with cheese, meat, or sweet fillings, baked in street ovens.
  • Grilled corn: charcoal-grilled and spiced — the perfect walking snack along the Nile corniche.

Essential Arabic Phrases for Food Lovers

  • "Shukran" — Thank you
  • "Kam dah?" — How much?
  • "Law samaht" — Please
  • "Mumkin [dish name]" — Can I have [dish name]
  • "Bidoun fulful" — Without chili (if you don't like spice)

Chapter 5: Egyptian Desserts & Traditional Drinks

Sweet Endings: Egypt's Dessert Culture

  • Umm Ali (National Dessert): layers of puff pastry soaked in sweetened milk with nuts, raisins, and coconut, baked until golden and bubbling. Served warm, straight from the baking dish.
  • Basbousa: moist semolina cake soaked in aromatic sugar syrup, topped with almonds or coconut. Sweet but not cloying, fragrant with rose or orange blossom water.
  • Konafa: shredded phyllo layered with sweet cheese or cream, baked crisp, then drenched in syrup. During Ramadan, konafa shops work around the clock.
  • Luqmat al-Qadi & Roz bel Laban: honey-soaked dough balls and comforting rice pudding — beloved everyday sweets.

Traditional Egyptian Drinks

  • Egyptian tea: strong and sweet, often with mint — "shai koshari" (golden) in the north, "shai sa'idi" (dark) in the south.
  • Turkish coffee: strong, unfiltered, served in small cups with grounds settled at the bottom.
  • Herbal infusions: hibiscus (karkade), anise (yansoon), mint (na'na'), and chamomile.
  • Ramadan classics: Sobia (coconut-rice drink) and Kamareddin (apricot nectar).
  • Winter specialty: Sahleb — a warming orchid-root drink with nuts and cinnamon.
  • Fresh juices: sugar cane, mango, guava, and orange from street juice stands.

Chapter 6: Dining Etiquette & Food Safety

Dining Customs and Etiquette

  • Right hand only: traditionally, eat with your right hand (the left is considered unclean).
  • Bread as utensil: scoop food with bread rather than requesting forks.
  • Compliment the host: praising the food generously is expected and appreciated.
  • Leave a little food: a completely clean plate might imply you're still hungry.
  • Tipping (baksheesh): expected in restaurants (5–10%) — see our complete tipping guide.
  • Home invitations: bring a small gift — sweets or flowers — if invited to an Egyptian home.

Food Safety & Health Considerations

  • Water: drink only bottled or purified water; avoid ice in drinks.
  • Street food: choose vendors with high turnover and visible cooking.
  • Fruits: peel all fruits yourself; avoid pre-cut fruit.
  • Seafood: ensure it's fresh and properly cooked, especially in hot months.
  • Spice tolerance: request "bidoun fulful" (without chili) if you're sensitive.
  • Traveler's diarrhea: common but usually mild — carry rehydration salts.

Seasonal Eating Guide

  • Spring (Mar–May): fresh strawberries, artichokes; Sham el-Nessim festival with feseekh (salted fish).
  • Summer (Jun–Aug): mango season, watermelon, light dishes and cold soups.
  • Autumn (Sep–Nov): date harvest, pomegranates; heartier stews return.
  • Winter (Dec–Feb): orange season, leafy greens, warming soups and stews.

Chapter 7: Food Photography in Egypt

Egypt's vibrant food scene offers extraordinary photographic opportunities. Our professional photographer guides provide hands-on instruction during all culinary experiences.

  • Essential gear: a fast prime lens (50mm f/1.8) for creamy bokeh, a 24–70mm zoom for environment shots, a portable reflector, and a polarizing filter to tame shiny surfaces.
  • Settings: aperture priority (f/2.8–f/5.6), low ISO 100–400, custom white balance for accurate food colors, burst mode for action shots.
  • Lighting: golden hour for street food, diffused window light indoors, and never harsh midday sun.
  • Composition: rule of thirds, leading lines from utensils, layered depth, and human elements — capture people preparing, serving, and enjoying food.
  • Storytelling: build sequences from market to table for a complete culinary narrative.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Egypt's national dish that I must try?
Koshari is widely considered Egypt's national dish. It is a comforting vegetarian meal made with rice, brown lentils, chickpeas, and macaroni, topped with spicy tomato sauce, garlic vinegar, and crispy fried onions.
Is Egyptian street food safe for tourists to eat?
Yes, Egyptian street food is generally safe when you choose busy vendors, eat freshly cooked hot food, avoid questionable raw vegetables, and drink bottled water only. Guided food tours can help you find reliable local vendors.
What are the best Egyptian dishes for vegetarians?
Excellent vegetarian options include Koshari, Ful Medames, Ta'ameya, Mulukhiyah without meat, Mahshi, Besarah, Baba Ghanoush, salads, and many traditional breakfast dishes.
Where can I find the most authentic Egyptian food experiences?
Authentic experiences are found in local eateries, street food markets, traditional homes, and guided culinary tours. Local guidance helps you avoid tourist traps and discover the best food neighborhoods.
What Egyptian desserts should I not miss?
Must-try desserts include Umm Ali, Basbousa, Konafa, Roz bel Laban, and Luqmat al-Qadi. Historic sweet shops and local bakeries are the best places to taste them.
