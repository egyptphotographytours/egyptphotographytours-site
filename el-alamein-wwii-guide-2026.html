El Alamein 2026: WWII Battlefields & Memorials

A sobering visit to the desert that changed World War II. Explore Commonwealth, German & Italian memorials with expert photography guidance.

Home/ Resources/ El Alamein 2026 Guide
Published January 10, 2026 Updated June 20, 2026 45 min read El Alamein, Egypt By Hossam, Photographer & History Enthusiast

Eighty-four years after the thunder of artillery faded over the desolate ridges of El Alamein, the silent white headstones and carefully preserved bunkers continue to draw thousands of visitors each year. For the military history enthusiast, the photographer seeking haunting landscapes, or the descendant of a veteran, El Alamein is not just a site — it is a pilgrimage. In 2026, with new visitor facilities and enhanced interpretation, the battlefield offers an even deeper connection to the events that turned the tide in North Africa during World War II.

This guide provides every detail you need for a meaningful visit, from the strategic importance of the battles to the exact locations of Commonwealth, German, and Italian memorials, and how to capture their solemn beauty through your lens.

Chapter 1: Why El Alamein Still Matters in 2026

El Alamein stands as a testament to the courage of soldiers from dozens of nations. Here, in the harsh desert, the Allies under General Bernard Montgomery halted the advance of Erwin Rommel's Afrika Korps. The battles were brutal, fought in extreme temperatures, with flies, dysentery, and the constant threat of tank warfare. Today, the landscape remains largely unchanged, offering a raw and authentic window into history.

The site's significance extends beyond military history; it represents the global nature of WWII, with troops from Britain, Australia, New Zealand, India, South Africa, Greece, Poland, and Free France fighting alongside each other against the Axis forces from Germany and Italy.

The Turning Point of North Africa

The battles at El Alamein were part of the larger Western Desert Campaign. The area's strategic value lay in its proximity to the Suez Canal, a vital artery for British supply lines to India and the Far East. The Allied victory here not only secured Egypt but also paved the way for the invasion of Italy and the eventual liberation of Europe.

"Before Alamein we never had a victory. After Alamein we never had a defeat."

— Winston Churchill

Chapter 2: The Geopolitical Context

World War II had engulfed Europe since 1939, with Germany's blitzkrieg tactics conquering much of the continent. In the Mediterranean, Italy, as Germany's ally, sought to revive the Roman Empire by expanding in Africa. Libya, an Italian colony since 1911, served as the launchpad for ambitions in Egypt.

Britain, controlling Egypt since 1882, viewed the region as crucial for its empire. The Suez Canal, opened in 1869, shortened the route to India by thousands of miles. Oil from the Middle East, vital for mechanized warfare, flowed through the area.

Key Historical Facts

  • September 1940: Italian invasion of Egypt begins
  • February 1941: Rommel arrives with the Afrika Korps
  • June 1942: Tobruk falls; Rommel pushes into Egypt
  • July 1942: First Battle of El Alamein halts Axis advance
  • October–November 1942: Second Battle of El Alamein — Allied victory

Chapter 3: The Battles of El Alamein

The First Battle (July 1–27, 1942)

Rommel, his supply lines stretched thin, attacked on July 1. His Panzerarmee Afrika faced the depleted Eighth Army. General Auchinleck took direct command, launching counterattacks that wore down the Axis forces. The battle ended in a stalemate, but it halted the Axis advance.

The Second Battle (October 23 – November 4, 1942)

Montgomery rebuilt the Eighth Army's morale and amassed superior forces: 220,000 men, 1,100 tanks, and 750 aircraft against Rommel's 115,000 men, 550 tanks, and 350 aircraft. The battle opened at 9:40 PM on October 23 with a massive artillery barrage from over 800 guns.

Battle Statistics

  • Allied Forces: 220,000 men, 1,100 tanks, 750 aircraft
  • Axis Forces: 115,000 men, 550 tanks, 350 aircraft
  • Allied Casualties: 13,500 killed/wounded
  • Axis Casualties: 30,000 killed/wounded, 30,000 captured

"The enemy is at the gate. We must fight here."

— Bernard Montgomery to his troops

Chapter 4: War Memorials & Cemeteries

Commonwealth War Cemetery

7,240 servicemen rest here. The Alamein Memorial bears the names of 11,866 men with no known grave. Designed by Sir Hubert Worthington and maintained by the Commonwealth War Graves Commission.

German War Memorial

An octagonal fortress-like structure of dark stone housing the remains of 4,280 German soldiers. The interior hall is somber, with a skylight illuminating the central slab.

Italian War Memorial

A striking white marble structure with a tall tower and central altar housing 4,800 Italian soldiers. The design is starkly beautiful with sweeping battlefield views.

Greek Memorial

Honors 600 fallen Greek soldiers with a temple-like design. South African and New Zealand memorials are integrated into the main cemetery.

Chapter 5: El Alamein Military Museum

Run by the Egyptian Ministry of Antiquities, the museum features indoor and outdoor exhibits: tanks (Sherman, Grant, Panzer IV, Italian Semovente), artillery pieces, aircraft wreckage, and personal items recovered from the battlefield. A new wing uses augmented reality to show troop movements.

Museum Highlights

  • Rommel's captured staff car
  • Montgomery's map table
  • Soldier letters and personal items
  • Over 100 vehicles in outdoor area
  • Interactive battle simulations

Chapter 6: Photography Guide for El Alamein

Photography Tips for Memorials

  • Golden hour: Sunrise and sunset provide warm light that adds depth to memorial architecture
  • Composition: Use repeating headstones as leading lines. Include the desert horizon for scale
  • Equipment: Wide-angle for interiors and panoramas, telephoto for details. Tripod allowed but be discreet
  • Ethics: Never stand on graves or touch artifacts. Ask permission if photographing people
  • Advanced Techniques: HDR for high-contrast scenes, long exposures for star trails over memorials
  • Filters: ND for bright days, UV for dust protection
  • Drone Rules: Prohibited without military permit; ground shots only

Chapter 7: Practical Visitor Information

Getting There

  • 120 km west of Alexandria (1.5 hrs drive)
  • 280 km from Cairo (3 hrs drive)
  • Guided tours or private car recommended

Opening Hours

  • Memorials: Daily 8am–4pm
  • Museum: 9am–5pm (closed 1–2pm)
  • Cemeteries always accessible

Entry Fees

  • Museum: E£100 (foreigners)
  • Cemeteries: Free
  • Best time: October–April

Safety Warning

Unexploded ordnance remains a real danger in open desert areas. Visitors must never wander off designated paths. Our guided tours ensure you explore only safe, cleared areas.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is El Alamein historically significant?
El Alamein was the site of two major battles in 1942 during WWII. The Second Battle of El Alamein marked a turning point in the North Africa campaign, halting the Axis advance towards Egypt and the Suez Canal. It was the first major land defeat for the German army under Rommel.
What memorials can I visit at El Alamein?
Visitors can explore the Commonwealth War Cemetery with 7,240 graves, the German War Memorial (an octagonal fortress-like structure), the Italian War Memorial with its striking marble altar, and the El Alamein Military Museum displaying tanks, artillery, and historical artifacts.
How do I get to El Alamein from Cairo or Alexandria?
El Alamein is about a 3-hour drive from Cairo (280 km) and 1.5 hours from Alexandria (120 km) via the northern coastal road. Most visitors join guided tours or hire a private car.
Are there any safety concerns when visiting the battlefields?
While the main memorials are safe, unexploded ordnance may still exist in open desert areas. Visitors must stay on marked paths and follow guide instructions.
Can I take photographs inside the cemeteries and museum?
Photography is permitted in all memorial sites and the museum for personal use. Tripods may require permission.
